# Experimental Design
- Strategy: ────────────────────────────────────────
Experiment 1 – Head-to-head fine-tuning on factual-correction data
Goal: Show that SOFT-FAR produces more factual and generally preferred answers than existing single-stage methods without extra compute.
Procedure:
1. Models: Llama-2-7B, Mistral-7B, Phi-2 (fresh SFT checkpoints).
2. Training sets: 17 k SPA + 10 k automatic fact-correction + 30 k ShareGPT-clean.
   • All methods get identical batches and see exactly one A100-hour of training (≈85 k steps at 4 × 16 tokens, fp16).
3. Systems compared: CE-only SFT, FIGA, SAFARI, DiscoPOP, DPO, and SOFT-FAR.
4. Evaluation suites (zero-shot):
   a) TruthfulQA MC-1 & generation (hallucination rate).
   b) FEVER / FActScore (evidence-based factuality).
   c) GPT-4 reward & BERTScore on 1 000 held-out ShareGPT prompts.
   d) Human pairwise (600 prompts, 3 annotators, majority vote).
5. Efficiency logging: wall-clock, GPU util, peak memory.
Expected outcome: 
• SOFT-FAR tops factuality metrics (≥+4 FActScore, ≥−12 % hallucinations vs FIGA). 
• Gains of +1–2 GPT-4 reward and ≥55 % human wins despite identical compute budget. 
• Throughput within 5 % of CE and ≥1.5× faster than DPO. 
Demonstrates: factual-aware OT alignment gives higher-quality, safer outputs at SFT cost.

────────────────────────────────────────
Experiment 2 – Many-to-many & re-ordering stress-test
Goal: Verify that the soft optimal-transport core really copes with complex rewrites where hard matching fails.
Procedure:
1. Construct 4 000 synthetic revision pairs following four patterns: (i) single-token replace, (ii) many-to-one merge, (iii) one-to-many split, (iv) reorder + paraphrase. Generate with Mixtral-8×7B and manually spot-check.
2. Fine-tune Llama-2-7B for 10 k steps with either SOFT-FAR, FIGA (Levenshtein) or SAFARI (cos-NN).
3. During training log: a) NEW-GOOD span recall (soft IoU), b) gradient L2 norm on revised tokens, c) perplexity drop on revised vs unchanged tokens.
4. After 10 k steps measure model probability assigned to gold good tokens (P_good) and to bad tokens that should disappear (P_bad).
Expected outcome:
• SOFT-FAR reaches NEW-GOOD recall ≈80 % on patterns (ii)–(iv) where FIGA/SAFARI stagnate ≤60 %.
• Gradient norms on merged / split tokens stay >0.2× baseline for SOFT-FAR but vanish for others → proves end-to-end differentiability.
• Final P_good↑, P_bad↓ largest for SOFT-FAR.
Demonstrates: OT alignment effectively trains on complex semantic edits that defeat hard-matching objectives.

────────────────────────────────────────
Experiment 3 – Cross-domain generalisation & safety
Goal: Test whether fact-aware weighting improves factuality in domains unseen during fine-tuning and does not hurt harmlessness.
Procedure:
1. Take the best checkpoints from Experiment 1.
2. Evaluation sets: 
   • Bio-Med QA (PubMedQA, MedMCQA),
   • FinanceQA,
   • Code-error-explanation (BugFixEval),
   • Harmlessness/Safety (Anthropic HH-RLHF subset).
3. Metrics: domain-specific accuracy, FActScore, model self-consistency, and harmlessness rate (adversarial jailbreak prompts).
4. Additionally compute representation distance W2(prompt, answer) before/after fine-tuning to examine whether SOFT-FAR over-specialises.
Expected outcome:
• Across all four new domains, SOFT-FAR boosts factual accuracy by 3–6 pp over SFT and 1–3 pp over DPO while keeping harmlessness equal or better.
• W2 distances remain comparable to SFT, indicating no over-fitting.
Demonstrates: fact-aware reweighting learns a generally useful alignment signal, not just dataset-specific shortcuts, and maintains safety.

information on almost every conceivable topic, we should have\nthe ability to collect facts that answer almost every conceivable question. However, only a small fraction of this\ninformation is contained in structured sources (Wikidata, Freebase, etc.) – we are therefore limited by our ability to\ntransform free-form text to structured knowledge. There is, however, another problem that has become the focus of a lot\nof recent research and media coverage: false information coming from unreliable sources.\n\nThe FEVER workshops are a venue for work in verifiable knowledge extraction and to stimulate progress in this direction.\n\n- FEVER Dataset: FEVER (Fact Extraction and VERification) consists of 185,445 claims generated by altering sentences\nextracted from Wikipedia and subsequently verified without knowledge of the sentence they were derived from. The claims\nare classified as Supported, Refuted or NotEnoughInfo. For the first two classes, the annotators also recorded the\nsentence(s) forming the necessary evidence for their judgment.\n\n- FEVER 2.0 Adversarial Attacks Dataset: The FEVER 2.0 Dataset consists of 1174 claims created by the submissions of\nparticipants in the Breaker phase of the 2019 shared task. Participants (Breakers) were tasked with generating\nadversarial examples that induce classification errors for the existing systems. Breakers submitted a dataset of up to\n1000 instances with equal number of instances for each of the three classes (Supported, Refuted NotEnoughInfo). Only\nnovel claims (i.e. not contained in the original FEVER dataset) were considered as valid entries to the shared task.\nThe submissions were then manually evaluated for Correctness (grammatical, appropriately labeled and meet the FEVER\nannotation guidelines requirements).\n\n### Supported Tasks and Leaderboards\n\nThe task is verification of textual claims against textual sources.\n\nWhen compared to textual entailment (TE)/natural language inference, the key difference is that in these tasks the\npassage to verify each claim is given, and in recent years it typically consists a single sentence, while in\nverification systems it is retrieved from a large set of documents in order to form the evidence.\n\n### Languages\n\nThe dataset is in English.\n\n## Dataset Structure\n\n### Data
